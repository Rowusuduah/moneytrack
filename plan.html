Paycheck & allocation

Why monthly is not two paychecks

You are paid every two weeks, not twice a month. That is 52 ÷ 2 = 26 paychecks a year. Multiplying by 2 assumes 24, which quietly loses two whole paychecks — $5,557.90 a year.

So $6,021.06 is the average month, correct for annual planning. It is not what lands in a typical month. Ten months a year you receive $5,557.90; twice a year you receive $8,336.85. Every biweekly figure in this plan is the real unit — the monthly column is a conversion for reading, not a paycheck.

How to actually invest it

You asked where 7% comes from and where to begin. Here is the honest answer to both, then the exact mechanics.

Where the 7% comes from

It is not a product anyone sells you. It is the long-run real return of the US stock market — what is left after inflation. Since 1928 the S&P 500 has returned about 10% a year nominally with dividends reinvested. Inflation has averaged about 3.1%. Subtract one from the other and you get roughly 6.7–7% real, which is the number I use throughout this plan.

That matters for reading every projection here: the numbers are in today's purchasing power. When the chart says $2.9M, it means $2.9M of what a dollar buys now — not an inflated future dollar that feels big and buys little. I set the default slider to 6.7% rather than 7% because 6.7% is what actually happened over the full 97-year record.

Nobody earns 7% every year. The market gave −37% in 2008 and +32% in 2013. The 7% is what the average comes to if you hold through all of it. The Scenarios tab tests exactly what happens if you are unlucky about when you start.

What to actually buy

One fund is enough. Genuinely. A total-market or S&P 500 index fund holds hundreds or thousands of companies, so no single failure can hurt you badly, and the fee is almost nothing. The choice below is nearly cosmetic — pick the one matching your brokerage and stop thinking about it.

The finding that matters

Nonresident tax

The part almost every F-1 investor gets wrong. While you file as a nonresident, your investments are taxed on entirely different rules — and Ghana has no US tax treaty to soften any of it.

What this changes
I earlier told you to favour liquid taxable brokerage over extra 401k. That was wrong in one direction. A taxable account loses 30% of every dividend and 30% of every realised gain while you are a nonresident. Your 401k loses none of it.

You defer 5% against a $24,500 limit — roughly $1,679 a month of unused shelter. The counter-argument is real: 401k money is locked to 59½, and leaving the US early means up to 30% withholding plus a 10% penalty. A genuine trade, not a free win.

The shape I would suggest, as a question for the CPA rather than a decision off a web page: fill reserves in cash first, since high-yield interest is US tax-free for you. Then lean toward the 401k while nonresident, and shift to taxable once the NIW lands. The nonresident drag is temporary; build to outlast it.
